  <h2>Per-question breakdown</h2>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>1. A) judicious</h3>
    <p>Clue: "acknowledged the strengths of every proposal before explaining, without rancor, why it favored one" — that is balanced, fair-minded judgment.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>B) caustic</b> — bitter, sharp; "without rancor" rules it out.</li>
      <li><b>C) indifferent</b> — the committee clearly cared enough to weigh every proposal.</li>
      <li><b>D) evasive</b> — it did state a preference and its reasons, so it was not dodging.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>2. B) haphazard</h3>
    <p>Clue: "Far from being ______, the novel's plot is <em>tightly controlled</em>." The blank is the opposite of tightly controlled — random, disorganized.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) intricate</b> — a tightly controlled plot can also be intricate, so this is not an opposite; the "far from being" frame needs a contrast.</li>
      <li><b>C) suspenseful</b> — not the opposite of controlled either.</li>
      <li><b>D) derivative</b> — about originality, a different axis entirely.</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="strat">"Far from being X, it is Y" means X and Y are opposites. Solve for the opposite of the stated half.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>3. C) tentative</h3>
    <p>Clue: "noting that a larger sample might well overturn them" — she is signaling that the findings are not yet firm.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) definitive, D) conclusive</b> — both mean settled beyond doubt, the opposite of what the second clause says.</li>
      <li><b>B) exhaustive</b> — means thorough in coverage; it does not address how firm the conclusions are.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>4. D) intensify</h3>
    <p>Clue: critics "were surprised by its restraint and quiet." For restraint to be a surprise, they must have expected <em>more</em> of the bombast, not less.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) abandon</b> — if they expected him to abandon the loud style, restraint would confirm the expectation, not surprise them.</li>
      <li><b>B) conceal</b> — you cannot conceal your own directorial style in this sense.</li>
      <li><b>C) satirize</b> — nothing suggests the film mocks his earlier work.</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="strat">When the sentence ends in a surprise, the blank must set up the expectation that the surprise violates.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>5. A) equivocal</h3>
    <p>Clue: "promised much but committed to nothing that could later be checked" — deliberately vague, open to more than one reading.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>B) blunt</b> — the opposite; blunt statements are direct and checkable.</li>
      <li><b>C) meticulous</b> — about care with detail, not vagueness.</li>
      <li><b>D) inflammatory</b> — provocative; the sentence describes caution, not provocation.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>6. B) complementary</h3>
    <p>Clue: "each accounting for cases the other leaves unexplained" — the theories fill each other's gaps, so they work together.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) identical</b> — if identical, they could not cover different cases.</li>
      <li><b>C) contradictory</b> — "appear to conflict" is set against the blank by "Though"; the real relationship is not conflict.</li>
      <li><b>D) obsolete</b> — nothing says either theory is outdated.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>7. C) streamline</h3>
    <p>Clue: "cutting three paragraphs of background" — making the argument leaner and more direct.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) expand</b> — the opposite of cutting.</li>
      <li><b>B) obscure</b> — cutting known background clarifies, it does not hide.</li>
      <li><b>D) abandon</b> — the writer keeps the argument; only background is removed.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>8. D) dismissed</h3>
    <p>Clue: "assigning a single student to survey it" plus "forced a dramatic reassessment" — they underrated the site at first.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) revered</b> — the opposite; you do not staff a revered site with one student.</li>
      <li><b>B) excavated, C) documented</b> — both describe careful archaeological work, which contradicts the minimal effort and the later "reassessment."</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>9. A) concision</h3>
    <p>Clue: "in ten pages it says what other writers take an entire book to say" — saying much in little space.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>B) ambiguity</b> — vagueness is a fault here, not the source of power.</li>
      <li><b>C) erudition</b> — deep learning; the sentence praises economy, not scholarship.</li>
      <li><b>D) urgency</b> — plausible in the abstract, but the length contrast points specifically to brevity.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>10. B) plodding</h3>
    <p>Clue: "weighed down by lists of dates and titles that never cohere" — slow, heavy, lifeless.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) scathing</b> — describes harsh criticism, not a dull book.</li>
      <li><b>C) speculative</b> — the problem is too many hard facts, not too much guessing.</li>
      <li><b>D) concise</b> — the opposite; the book is bogged down, not tight.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>11. C) probity</h3>
    <p>Clue: "she was known never to misrepresent one party's position to the other" — that is strict honesty. <span class="word">Probity</span> means integrity, uprightness.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) tenacity</b> — persistence; not what builds trust here.</li>
      <li><b>B) charisma</b> — personal charm; the sentence is about truthfulness.</li>
      <li><b>D) flexibility</b> — willingness to compromise; unrelated to not lying.</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="strat">Hard word, easy clue. The "known never to misrepresent" phrase defines the blank for you — trust the definition, not your comfort with the word.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>12. D) choreographed</h3>
    <p>Clue: "every line timed and rehearsed for weeks" — planned and arranged in advance, in contrast to the "spontaneous" appearance.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) improvised</b> — the exact thing the sentence says it only <em>looks</em> like.</li>
      <li><b>B) abridged</b> — shortened; nothing about length here.</li>
      <li><b>C) transcribed</b> — written down after the fact; the planning happens before.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>13. A) inseparable from</h3>
    <p>Clue: "the awkward perspective was exactly what gave the scene its unsettling force" — the flaw and the achievement are the same feature.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>B) fatal to</b> — the opposite; the flaw makes the painting work.</li>
      <li><b>C) unrelated to</b> — the sentence links them tightly.</li>
      <li><b>D) exaggerated by</b> — does not fit; the flaw is not making the achievement seem bigger, it <em>is</em> the achievement.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>14. B) derided</h3>
    <p>Clue: "who mocked its reliance on the idea of drifting continents" — "mocked" defines the blank directly. <span class="word">Derided</span> = ridiculed.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) celebrated</b> — contradicts "mocked."</li>
      <li><b>C) funded</b> — off-topic; the sentence is about reputation, not money.</li>
      <li><b>D) tolerated</b> — too mild; "mocked" is active scorn, not grudging acceptance.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="rat">
    <h3>15. C) comprehensiveness</h3>
    <p>Clue: "gathered over forty years of uninterrupted observation" — the data's value is its scope and completeness.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><b>A) brevity</b> — forty years of data is the opposite of brief.</li>
      <li><b>B) novelty</b> — the sentence contrasts the familiar conclusions with the data; nothing says the data is new in kind.</li>
      <li><b>D) inconsistency</b> — a defect; the sentence is praising the data.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
